# Test creator cohorts first, then sell to employers

Aurelia's most promising B2B2C route is **creator-led cohorts, but not at the assumptions in Eugene's Retreat-in-a-Box spec**. Creators bring buyers at close to zero media cost to Aurelia, and that matters because paid acquisition for a wellness subscription costs roughly $100–$400 per payer against about $36 of median first-year payer revenue in Health & Fitness. The spec's headline numbers do not survive contact with the evidence, though. **76.5% still active at week 8 is about 16 times the measured day-30 retention of mindfulness apps (4.7%)**, a 60/40 split is far richer for the platform than the 0–10% creators pay on Kajabi, Maven or Teachable, and **Aurelia's 40% of a €19.99 web ticket comes to about €6.45, which premium AI voice generation for 24 sessions can wipe out on its own**. Employers are the second real channel. A content-only app sits at about $50–70 per seat per year (Wysa, Calm Teams), not at the "double digits per member per month" that clinical-care vendors charge, and buyers now judge renewal on activation dashboards. Insurers and health systems are closed to Aurelia until it has clinical evidence, and pursuing that evidence would turn Aurelia into a regulated medical device. Of the partner channels, the evidence favours deals where the end user enters their own card: trial codes that renew at retail, carrier billing, and wearable data integrations. Free placements on airline seatbacks and hotel TVs mostly lapsed. Underneath every channel sits a compliance floor that is not optional. The spec's "+85.7% sleep improvement" and "stress −50%" are invented figures and cannot be used in any market, creators' social posts can turn the product into a medical device under UK guidance, and a free-text "how I feel" input guarantees crisis disclosures. The first test should be a small, instrumented, digital-only cohort pilot with three to five creators, judged on fill rate, week-by-week activity and Plus conversion per ticket buyer rather than per graduate.

## Paid acquisition cannot pay back, which is why B2B2C matters

The case for any B2B2C model starts with the arithmetic of going it alone. In RevenueCat's 2025 data across more than 115,000 apps, the median Health & Fitness app converts **6.9% of downloads into trials, 37.7% of trials into paid, and 2.9% of downloads into payers by day 35**, and freemium apps across all categories convert only about 2.1% against 10.7% for hard paywalls ([RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)). Paid-social installs for fitness and wellness cost about $1.50–$3.50 on TikTok and $2.50–$5.00 on Meta ([Semnexus 2026](https://semnexus.com/app-user-acquisition-benchmarks-by-vertical-2026)), and fully loaded acquisition cost typically runs two to three times the install price ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/customer-acquisition-cost/)). Divide a $3–5 install by a 2.9% install-to-paid rate and each payer costs **about $100–$170 in media alone, or $200–$400 fully loaded**. Against that, the median Health & Fitness yearly payer brings in **$35.64 of realised revenue in year one** ([RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)).

Aurelia's prices sit at the top of the category. The market clusters at $60–$80 a year: Headspace charges $69.99 a year and $12.99 a month ([Breethe](https://breethe.com/sleep-and-meditation-app-guide/compare-evaluate/how-much-does-breethe-cost-compared-to-calm-and-headspace-a-real-world-price-breakdown)), Insight Timer about $59.99, and only Waking Up reaches about $99.99–$129.99 ([Breethe 2024](https://admin.breethe.com/sleep-and-meditation-app-guide/compare-evaluate/which-mindfulness-app-offers-the-best-yearly-value-a-2024-price-comparison)). Aurelia Plus at $100 a year is about 40% above Calm and Headspace and 2.5 times the Health & Fitness median annual price of $39.94. Annual payers renew at about 44% after year one, against 17% for monthly payers ([RevenueCat 2025](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2025)), and Adapty puts the first renewal at 59.2%, falling to 45.1% and 37.1% at later renewals ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/health-fitness-app-subscription-benchmarks/)). An annual Aurelia payer is therefore worth about 1.8–2.0 paid years. After a 15% store fee and 20% EU VAT, a $10 store-billed month nets about $7.08, and a $100 year nets roughly $70, so the lifetime value is about **$125–$140 before AI costs**. That is less than a fully loaded paid-media payer costs. RevenueCat also finds that AI-powered apps earn 41% more per payer but **churn 30% faster** ([RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)), which weighs on an AI-native product.

The other structural cost is generation. Writing the script is close to free, at about $0.001 per ten-minute session on Haiku 5.5 or about $0.03 on Sonnet 5 ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Voice dominates. OpenAI's tts-1 costs $15 per million characters and tts-1-hd $30 ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)), while ElevenLabs reportedly charges $0.04–$0.08 per thousand characters ([Wireflow](https://www.wireflow.ai/blog/elevenlabs-api-pricing)). On a 6,500-character script, a personalised session costs **about $0.10 at the budget end and $0.55 at the premium end**. A Plus user generating a session every day costs $3–$16.50 a month against about $7 of net monthly revenue. Every channel below inherits this constraint: heavy use on premium voices loses money, and the margin depends on voice choice, reuse and caps.

## Retreat-in-a-Box works only with different assumptions

The creator-cohort model answers the acquisition problem, because the creator's audience is the marketing. Its weakness is that almost every number in the spec is optimistic, and the optimism compounds.

### The split asks creators to give up far more than the market charges

The going rate tracks who brings the customer. When the creator brings the buyer, platforms take little: Maven keeps 10% ([Maven guidebook](https://help.maven.com/en/articles/12240853-maven-instructor-policy-guidebook)), Patreon 10% for new creators ([Patreon](https://support.patreon.com/hc/en-us/articles/36426991446797)), Teachable 7.5% on its starter plan and 0% above it ([Teachable](https://www.teachable.com/pricing)), and Circle 0.5–2% ([Circle](https://circle.so/pricing)). When the platform brings the audience, the split shifts. Insight Timer pays teachers a pooled share of subscription revenue by play count and publishes no percentage ([Insight Timer](https://insight-timer.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/67000664874-how-can-teachers-earn-revenue-from-their-work-)); review sites claim it is about 50% ([ChoosingTherapy](https://www.choosingtherapy.com/insight-timer-review/)). In Retreat-in-a-Box the creator supplies the buyers, yet Aurelia asks for 40%, so creators will compare 60% with the 87–97% they keep selling a similar programme on a course tool. Aurelia can justify the gap only with things a course tool cannot do: personalised sessions in the creator's style, voice and sound, check-ins, and in-app demand. If it cannot, expect pressure towards 70/30 or 80/20, and a demand for a recurring share of the Plus subscriptions the creator drives. Insight Timer pays on ongoing plays and even pays bonuses for next-day return rates, which sets that expectation.

The price compounds the problem. At a full cohort, the creator nets about **€1,935 per 8-week cohort** (200 × €9.68 after VAT and fees). For a creator with 50,000 engaged followers, that is small next to a €99–€299 programme sold at 90%. Small creators would find it attractive, but small creators cannot fill 200 seats. No credible "share of followers who buy" benchmark exists. One Kit creator with about 9,000 subscribers sold 2–3 low-priced products per weekly email ([Kit](https://kit.com/resources/blog/gannon-meyer-instagram-to-email-automation)), and by our own rough arithmetic, 200 seats implies an engaged email list in the tens of thousands or a social following in the hundreds of thousands. Maven itself pivoted from "creators" to "experts" in 2022 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/13/mavens-a16z-backed-teaching-platform-pivots-from-creators-to-experts/amp/)), a sign of how hard creator-led cohort supply is to sustain. Recruiting 50 creators who can each fill 200 seats is a cold-start problem of its own.

### 76.5% at week 8 is a target, not a forecast

Cohort courses are famous for high completion, but the famous figures come from expensive, live, selective programmes and are unaudited. Maven claims 75–90% ([Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/posts/maven-eca0ac99-b076-4f60-8cb7-4d204fe8ff7f)), and altMBA's 96% traces to secondary sources for a programme costing thousands of dollars. Measured data is far lower. HarvardX and MITx MOOCs had a **median completion of 12.6%**, and fee-paying certificate learners top out at **about 60%** ([IRRODL 2019](https://www.erudit.org/fr/revues/irrodl/2019-v20-n3-irrodl04799/1062521ar.pdf)). For mental-health apps specifically, panel data on 93 apps found **median day-30 retention of 3.3%, and 4.7% for mindfulness apps** ([Baumel et al., JMIR 2019](https://www.jmir.org/2019/9/e14567)). Even Noom saw engagement with its programme fall in real-world use compared with trials ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8663454)). A €19.99, mostly asynchronous AI programme for 200 people resembles cheap self-paced learning more than altMBA. The ticket, the creator's accountability and the cohort should lift retention well above free-app baselines, but **20–50% active at week 8 is a defensible planning range**. That range is our judgement, not a benchmark. The spec's 76.5% should be traced to its source: if it came from a trial, it measures people answering follow-ups, not weekly use.

The 30% graduate-to-Plus rate is plausible taken on its own. It sits below the Health & Fitness median trial-to-paid rate of 37.7% ([RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)) and below Adapty's 42.2% ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/health-fitness-app-subscription-benchmarks/)), and a graduate who paid and stayed eight weeks is a strongly selected buyer. No published benchmark exists for paid programmes converting into subscriptions, however, and the conversion is a step up in price (€19.99 once, then $10 a month), not "keep what you have". The hidden variable is the denominator. Plus conversion should be reported **per ticket buyer**, because completion decides it.

Sold as a 15% in-app purchase, Aurelia's share falls to about €5.66; at Apple's full 30%, lower still. Plus revenue uses about $70 net in year one. Three conclusions follow. First, the ticket share is too small to carry personalised generation. At premium voice prices, AI cost per buyer ($13) is double Aurelia's ticket share (about €6.45), so the retreat has to run on a **pre-generated creator script and voice produced once, with short personalised inserts**, not 24 bespoke sessions per buyer. Second, the model's value sits almost entirely in Plus conversion. Each graduate is worth about $21 of expected first-year Plus revenue at Aurelia's near-zero acquisition cost, which beats paid media by an order of magnitude even in the planning case. Third, a full 50-creator wave at spec assumptions yields about €64,500 to Aurelia in ticket share and about 2,300 Plus subscribers. The planning case halves the subscribers, and the realistic risk is that most creators never fill 200 seats.

Two leaks are missing from the spec. Refunds on courses run about 2–10% depending on source ([Learning Revolution](https://www.learningrevolution.net/creator-tools/refund-rate-cost-calculator/)), Health & Fitness app refunds run 4.71% ([RevenueCat 2025](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2025)), and Maven refunds in full until a course's halfway point. EU consumers also have a 14-day withdrawal right unless they expressly consent to early supply ([Directive 2011/83/EU](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02011L0083-20220528)). The "box" should also stay digital. Wellness fulfilment costs **$8.85–$18.25 per order before product** ([Fulfyld](https://www.fulfyld.com/data/wellness-healthcare-fulfillment-benchmarks/)), which is 40–90% of the ticket. A physical kit only works as a separately priced add-on.

## Employers pay $50–70 a seat; insurers and clinics are closed doors

The employer channel is real, but Aurelia would enter at the cheap end. Clinical-care vendors quote privately and bundle therapy and coaching. Headspace describes a flat per-employee fee or a lower base plus billed sessions, with a three-year example contract, and says many competitors charge "double digits per member per month" ([Headspace](https://organizations.headspace.com/blog/evaluating-mental-health-solution-pricing-models)). Content-only apps are the relevant anchor. **Wysa lists $50 per employee per year** for code-based access ([Wysa for Work](https://www.wysa.com/wysa-for-work-copy)), and Calm Teams is reportedly about $70 per seat per year for groups of 5–300, with custom quotes above that ([FrontDeskReview](https://frontdeskreview.com/software/meditation-apps/calm/); [Calm](https://www.calm.com/teams)). A self-serve team tier at that price is how a young product earns its first B2B revenue without a sales team.

Activation is what decides renewals. In a peer-reviewed study of 950 employees enrolled in one employer digital mental-health benefit, **only 38% used any self-directed digital resource and about 44% never opened the app** ([JMIR 2024](https://jmir.org/2024/1/e48696)). EAP use on eligible populations is often cited at 2–3% ([Spring Health](https://www.springhealth.com/blog/how-innovative-eaps-drive-utilization)), and one heavily promoted case reached 28% ([Intellect](https://intellect.co/read/workplace-wellbeing-in-singapore-asean/)). Buyers are watching. Business Group on Health warns that vendors "must demonstrate value or face replacement" and found employers will use well-being dashboards to judge them ([BGH](https://www.businessgrouphealth.org/Newsroom/News-and-Press-Releases/Press-Releases/2025-Employer-Well-being-Strategy-Survey)). Employers expect costs to rise a median 9.2% in 2027 ([BGH 2027](https://www.businessgrouphealth.org/-/media/bgh/images/2027-employer-healthcare-strategy-survey/2027_hcs_survey-es-final.pdf)), and employer mental-health spending fell 7% in 2025 even as use rose ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/benefits/mental-health-utilization-is-up--but-employer-spending-fell-7-in-2025-587992.aspx)). Aurelia's credible pilot kit is a security and privacy pack (a data-processing agreement, GDPR or Indonesian PDP compliance, an ISO 27001 roadmap like Unmind's and Wysa's), an aggregate dashboard with minimum group sizes, and a crisis-escalation policy. Its pitch is activation: one sentence produces a session, which is a lower barrier than browsing a library. That claim is untested and is exactly what a pilot should measure against the eligible population.

Southeast Asia is under-served rather than empty. In Singapore, 45% of workers say their employer offers no EAP ([TELUS Health](https://go.telushealth.com/hubfs/MHI%202025/Q4/Singapore%20MHI%20September%202025.pdf)), and Indonesia's Riliv runs a consumer app beside a "Riliv for Company" programme ([DailySocial](https://en.dailysocial.id/post/the-role-of-Indonesian-mental-health-service-startups)). Budgets are smaller, though. Consumer trial-to-paid in India and Southeast Asia is **15.2%, against 34.2% in North America** ([RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)), and Adapty's price index puts India at 0.3 of the UK level ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/health-fitness-app-subscription-benchmarks/)). Aurelia would need local prices of roughly $25–35 a year there.

Insurers are not a near-term option. US payers bundle wellness apps as cancellable perks, not reimbursed benefits. Kaiser says Calm and Headspace "are not covered under your health plan benefits… and may be discontinued at any time" ([Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/mental-health/tools-resources/digital)), and the plans that bundle chose category leaders. Calm Health claims more than 39 million covered lives ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/chat/h/bfb4f0b2-424d-40e3-91dc-20f88806e16b/)). Real reimbursement exists in Germany's DiGA scheme, but only for certified medical devices. Average list prices of €541 fall about 50% in negotiation to a median of €221, and most DiGAs overrun their one-year trial window ([JMIR 2024](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e59013)); only 22% moved from trial to permanent listing ([GKV-Spitzenverband](https://www.gkv-spitzenverband.de/media/dokumente/krankenversicherung_1/telematik/digitales/2025_DiGA_Bericht_GKV_SV.pdf)). The NHS route needs DTAC and NICE assessment, and NICE rated even Wysa "research only" for lack of evidence ([NICE HTE8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/HTE8/chapter/3-committee-discussion)). The category's history makes the lesson concrete. Pear Therapeutics went from a $1.6 billion valuation to a **$6.05 million asset sale** because payers would not reimburse ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/pro/health-tech-deals/2023/05/22/pear-therapeutics-sold-6m-four-parts)). Headspace, valued at about $3 billion at its 2021 merger, agreed in 2026 to sell to Sword Health for **up to $300 million** ([HLTH](https://hlth.com/insights/news/sword-to-acquire-headspace-in-deal-worth-up-to-300m)). Calm's estimated ARR fell from about $450 million to about $300 million before it pivoted to B2B2C ([Sacra](https://www.sacra.com/c/calm)). Sword buying Headspace for an "AI care" platform is weak evidence that AI-personalised delivery has strategic value. The stronger lesson is to avoid models that need reimbursement or fixed clinical labour before revenue.

Public evidence on partner channels is almost all announcements, with no disclosed conversion rates, wholesale prices or churn after a bundle ends. That absence is itself a finding. The best available signal is whether partners renewed, and it splits cleanly by deal design.

The channels most likely to create durable payers are the ones where the end user enters their own payment method: trial codes that renew at retail, carrier-billed add-ons, and data integrations that require the app's own subscription. Free-for-all placements (seatbacks, hotel TVs, free Calm on Samsung TVs until May 2021 ([Samsung](https://samsung.com/us/support/answer/ANS10005231))) generated reach and then lapsed. Whole-base telco perks sit in between. They bring big reach but carrier-funded "subscribers" who disappear if the deal is not renewed, and video-streaming data showing bundles cut churn ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/ja-jp/publications/2024/why-video-streamers-need-to-rebundle)) says nothing about what happens when the bundle ends. For a pre-revenue app, the low-risk structures need no wholesale price: trial codes at retail, revenue share on carrier billing, and a wearable integration. Aurelia's onboarding already asks about wearables, so it lines up with the newest of these, the Oura model. Two openings deserve a targeted look. Luxury retreats such as Six Senses follow up with people at home through human programmes, not a third-party app ([Six Senses](https://www.sixsenses.com/en/wellness-spa/personalized-wellness)), so "your retreat continues as chapters" is unserved. In Indonesia, no global meditation app has a carrier or super-app bundle, Telkomsel is already building a health bundle with Rey ([Telkomsel](https://www.telkomsel.com/en/enterprise/insight/news/rey-and-telkomsel-launch-teman-series)), and Headspace reviews ask for Indonesian content.

## Creator copy, health data and crisis handling set the compliance floor

Every B2B2C model hands part of Aurelia's voice to someone else, and in this category that is where the legal risk sits. In the EU, UK, US and Indonesia, the line between wellness software and a medical device is drawn by **intended purpose as shown in claims and promotion**, not by technology. The MHRA's 2025 guidance treats "supports poor sleep at sub-clinical levels, for well-being only" as non-medical, but "helps improve sleep and can prevent depression and anxiety" as medical. Its "inferred medical purpose" example applies when a website says well-being but **social media suggests the product reduces depression risk**: the maker must either regulate the product as a device or remove those inferences ([MHRA DMHT guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6866572fadfe29730ea3a9d5/MHRA_guidance_on_DMHT_-_Device_characterisation_regulatory_qualification_and_classification.pdf)). The same guidance rates generative AI as "high" functionality and treats PHQ-9 or GAD-7 check-ins as a clinical signal. In a creator model, a creator who frames "bad to good" as healing anxiety is therefore a device-classification risk as well as an advertising one. The FDA's January 2026 general-wellness guidance keeps relaxation and stress-management claims outside device regulation, but only as enforcement discretion ([Covington](https://cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products)), and Indonesia's KMK 951/2026 now governs AI-based medical software by "function and product claims" ([SIP Law Firm](https://siplawfirm.id/id/publikasi/alat-kesehatan-berbasis-software-kepmenkes-951-2026)).

The spec's outcome claims cannot be used. Quantified health claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence held before the claim is made, under FTC health-claims guidance ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)) and UK CAP Code rule 12.1 ([CAP Code](https://www.asa.org.uk/static/c6be0fb9-2c66-4248-ba5b824bf26fd3d3/fa3714eb-f3e9-4c45-b9d31f1b87d00d3a/The-CAP-Code.pdf)). The ASA has already ruled against an AI mental-health app, using MHRA guidance to decide that its ads implied clinical effect ([ASA, Antipadia](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/antipadia-enterprises-ltd.html)). It has also ruled that an app's sleep claims needed substantiation ([ASA, Oakever](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/oakever-games-pte-ltd-g24-1257433-oakever-games-pte-ltd.html)). "+85.7% sleep improvement" and "stress −50%" come from Aurelia's mock data, which makes them deceptive per se in real marketing. AI-generated member stories would breach the FTC's Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule, in force since October 2024 with penalties of **$53,088 per violation** ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials); [Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2025/12/ftc-signals-heightened-enforcement-of-new)). The brand carries its creators' liability. The FTC Endorsement Guides make advertisers liable for endorsers' misleading statements, and the EU's proposed Digital Fairness Act would require brands to ensure "contractually or by other appropriate means" that influencers comply, with fines reported up to 6% of turnover ([Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/11/11/european-commission-publishes-long-awaited-digital-fairness-act-102o9b5)). The creator contract therefore needs pre-approved claims, mandatory paid-partnership disclosure, a ban on medical framing and on recommending the programme to patients, takedown rights and an indemnity.

Health data is the second exposure. A sentence about how someone feels is health data under GDPR Article 9, "consumer health data" under Washington's My Health My Data Act, which carries a private right of action and saw its first class action in 2025 ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250220-first-lawsuit-filed-under-washingtons-my-health-my-data-act)), and specific personal data under Indonesia's PDP Law ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/int/legal-updates/compliance-with-indonesia-s-personal-data-protection-law-by-october-2024)). US enforcement follows one pattern: sharing health signals with ad platforms or partners without express consent. That pattern cost BetterHelp $7.8 million ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/teladoc-owned-betterhelp-pay-78m-users-alleged-data-misuse-ftc-order)), GoodRx $1.5 million ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/b/ftc-proposes-1-5m-fine-for-violation-of-health-breach-notification-rule)) and Cerebral about $7 million, part of it for cancellation practices ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/proposed-ftc-order-will-prohibit-telehealth-firm-cerebral-using-or-disclosing-sensitive-data)). The design rules follow directly. Creators and employers get **aggregate, de-identified metrics only**. No ad pixel or SDK runs on any screen that captures feelings. Employer reporting uses minimum group sizes. Since 2 February 2025 the EU AI Act has banned AI that infers emotions in workplaces ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)), so Aurelia must never market itself as a way to read staff wellbeing, and must keep any wearable-based mood inference out of employer deployments. Article 50 has required AI disclosure and machine-readable marking of synthetic audio since August 2026.

Crisis handling is the third. Users will type self-harm content into a free-text feelings box. California's SB 243, in force since January 2026, requires a suicide-prevention protocol with crisis referral and annual reporting ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/13/california-becomes-first-state-to-regulate-ai-companion-chatbots)). Illinois and Nevada ban AI that provides or represents itself as providing therapy, with fines up to $10,000 and $15,000 ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-ca/insights/publications/2025/08/ai-mental-health-chatbots)), and Character.AI and Google settled the teen-suicide suits in January 2026 ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/character-ai-google-agree-to-settle-teen-chatbot-harm-lawsuits)). A creator's persona must never override the safety flow or present as a clinician. Finally, splitting payments through Stripe Connect and presenting the sessions under Aurelia's brand makes Aurelia the trader in practice. EU VAT rules presume the platform is the supplier ([Implementing Regulation 282/2011](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02011R0282-20250101)), and the DSA removes the hosting exemption where the platform appears to supply the item itself ([DSA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj)). Refunds, withdrawal and VAT should therefore be run centrally, with Aurelia as merchant of record.

## What to test first, and the numbers that decide it

The first test should be a **paid, digital-only Retreat pilot with three to five creators**, deliberately small so that every number is observed rather than assumed. Each cohort runs on a pre-generated creator script and voice with short personalised inserts, keeping AI cost below about €2 per buyer. Tickets sell through web checkout, with Aurelia as merchant of record and the EU early-supply consent captured. Before the first ticket sells, Aurelia needs a wellness-only intended-purpose statement, an approved-claims list with none of the mock percentages, the creator contract described above, a crisis-detection and referral flow, an age gate and no ad pixels. The pilot should measure five things against thresholds set in advance. Suggested thresholds, which are our judgement rather than benchmarks: a median cohort fill of at least 100 paid seats, at least 35% active at week 8, Plus conversion of at least 8% of ticket buyers, refunds below 10%, and AI plus payment cost below a third of Aurelia's ticket share. The pilot should also test the split, by offering at least one creator 70/30 plus a share of the Plus revenue they generate, since that is the counter-offer creators are most likely to make. Missing fill means the creators' reach is the constraint. Missing week-8 activity means the product is the constraint. Missing Plus conversion means the cohort is a one-off event rather than a funnel. Each points to a different fix.

The pilot also forces work the product does not yet have. Today the app runs on mock data with no backend, and sign-in deliberately does not persist, so a paid cohort needs real accounts, payments, consented data storage and the aggregate creator dashboard. That build also serves every later channel. In parallel, and cheaply, Aurelia can run a **self-serve team tier at about $50–70 a seat a year** with two or three small employers, reporting activation and 30-day retention against eligible staff, because those are the numbers every employer buyer now asks for. It can also run **one trial-code partnership** with a boutique studio, retreat operator or gift buyer, where codes convert at retail with a card on file. A wearable integration and an Indonesian carrier-billing pitch come next, once there is retention data to show. Insurers, DiGA, NHS routes and any clinical claim come last, if ever. They require a deliberate decision to become a medical device, and the incumbents' write-downs show how expensive that road is.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "which channel" to "who pays for acquisition, and who owns the card". Creator cohorts are attractive because the creator pays for acquisition, but in the current design Aurelia's 40% is too large for creators to accept and too small for Aurelia to cover per-user AI generation. The model works only as a cheap, mostly pre-produced funnel into Plus, which makes Plus conversion per ticket buyer the single number that decides the plan. That conversion has no public benchmark, so a small pilot creates information nobody else has published.

The second insight is that Aurelia's distinctive feature, a free-text sentence about feelings, is both its activation advantage and its main liability. It is what could beat the 38% activation of employer benefits, and it is also what makes the input health data, attracts crisis disclosures, and lets one creator's caption turn a wellness app into an unregistered medical device. The partnerships that last in this category kept the end user's payment relationship and kept claims in wellness terms. If Aurelia does the same, it can test its riskiest assumptions on a few hundred real buyers before 50 creators repeat them in public.

# Regulatory, health-claims, safety and contract risks of selling an AI wellness programme through third parties (creators, employers, partners)

Researched 2026-11-23. This is not legal advice. Each item is dated and given a status. Sources marked "(primary text, not re-fetched this session)" are well-known primary documents cited from their canonical URLs; this session did not retrieve them, so check the current text before relying on them. All other items came from search results or documents retrieved on 2026-11-23.

Product context: the user writes one sentence about how they feel, and AI generates a guided session (voice, sound, script). The proposal is an 8-week "bad to good" retreat sold by creators at €19.99 on a 60/40 split, with the creator's "philosophy" steering the AI. The spec shows claims such as "+85.7% sleep improvement" and "stress −50%". Target markets are the EU, UK, US and Indonesia/SEA.

## Wellness vs medical device (EU MDR, UK MHRA, US FDA, Indonesia Kemenkes/BPOM): when does the app become SaMD, and which claims cross the line?

### Takeaway
In all four jurisdictions the line is drawn by **intended purpose as shown in claims, labelling and promotion, including social media**, not by the technology. "Supports relaxation / better everyday sleep / sub-clinical stress" stays on the wellness side. Treating, preventing or diagnosing insomnia, anxiety or depression, or targeting people with a diagnosed condition, makes the software a medical device. Under MHRA guidance, a creator's social posts can turn a wellness product into a medical device. In the B2B2C model, creator marketing is therefore a device-classification risk as well as an advertising risk.

### Cited Findings
**UK (MHRA)**
- The MHRA published "Digital mental health technology: qualification and classification" in February 2025 (the PDF carries version history; this note reads v1.3). The guidance covers device characterisation, then SaMD qualification, then classification. It is part of a three-year MHRA/NICE project funded by Wellcome. — [gov.uk publication page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-mental-health-technology-qualification-and-classification); [press release](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/digital-mental-health-technologies-guidance-launched-to-help-manufacturers-and-safeguard-users); [PDF v1.3](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6866572fadfe29730ea3a9d5/MHRA_guidance_on_DMHT_-_Device_characterisation_regulatory_qualification_and_classification.pdf)
- The guidance gives worked examples of intended-purpose statements. **Example 1 (NOT a medical purpose):** "This product supports treatment of poor sleep. It is only for well-being and when poor sleep would be considered at sub-clinical levels, rather than clinical levels as part of a diagnosable mental health condition." **Example 2 (medical purpose):** poor sleep "at sub-clinical levels or clinical levels as part of a diagnosable mental health condition such as insomnia disorder, major depressive disorder or generalised anxiety disorder." **Example 3 (medical purpose):** "helps improve sleep and can prevent common mental health conditions such as depression and anxiety." **Example 4 (medical purpose):** "reduces stress for those with epilepsy" (wellbeing aimed at people with another diagnosed condition). — [MHRA DMHT guidance PDF, §6.1.3](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6866572fadfe29730ea3a9d5/MHRA_guidance_on_DMHT_-_Device_characterisation_regulatory_qualification_and_classification.pdf)
- **Example 6, "inferred medical purpose":** the website says "well-being", but the manufacturer's social media suggests the product reduces the risk of depression, or GPs recommend it for moderate depression. The manufacturer must then either regulate it as a device or "remove any inferences to mental health conditions on social media and elsewhere and ensure it is not being recommended by healthcare professionals." — [MHRA DMHT guidance PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6866572fadfe29730ea3a9d5/MHRA_guidance_on_DMHT_-_Device_characterisation_regulatory_qualification_and_classification.pdf)
- Other signals the MHRA treats as clinical intent: promotional materials, including adverts and social media, that point to diagnosable conditions; standardised measures such as **PHQ-9 or GAD-7** where users with any score can carry on using the product; and use or recommendation within an NHS service. Products aimed at "symptoms of depression", "low mood" or "worrying" are in scope where this could include people at clinically relevant levels. — [MHRA DMHT guidance PDF, Table 2 Note 1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6866572fadfe29730ea3a9d5/MHRA_guidance_on_DMHT_-_Device_characterisation_regulatory_qualification_and_classification.pdf)
- The MHRA's functionality table rates a **generative AI chatbot** that collects symptoms as "High" functionality ("Processes data / information using AI"), compared with a fixed form rated "Low". High functionality pushes a product with a medical purpose towards SaMD. — [MHRA DMHT guidance PDF, Table 3](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6866572fadfe29730ea3a9d5/MHRA_guidance_on_DMHT_-_Device_characterisation_regulatory_qualification_and_classification.pdf)
- A newer revision PDF exists on gov.uk, and the MHRA DMHT working group planned to discuss possible updates to the qualification guidance in February 2026. — [newer PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a68c3490825cc51a6c37c1d/Digital_mental_health_technology_device_characterisation__regulatory_qualification_and_classification.pdf); [DMHT working group](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/role-of-the-digital-mental-health-technology-dmht-working-group)

**EU (MDR 2017/745)**
- MDCG 2019-11 is the EU guidance on qualifying and classifying software under the MDR. Software with a medical purpose (diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of disease) is a device. Rule 11 classifies most such software as class IIa or higher. The MHRA guidance's EU section was updated to follow "MDCG 2019-11 Rev 1". — [MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_qualification_classification_software_en_0.pdf) (primary text, not re-fetched this session); [MHRA PDF version history](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6866572fadfe29730ea3a9d5/MHRA_guidance_on_DMHT_-_Device_characterisation_regulatory_qualification_and_classification.pdf)

**US (FDA)**
- On 6 January 2026 the FDA issued a revised final "General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices" guidance, which **supersedes the 2019 version** (that in turn replaced the 2016 original). It was issued without a comment period. The two-part test is unchanged: the product is intended *only* for general wellness, and it presents low risk. The main new content concerns non-invasive sensors that estimate blood pressure, SpO2 or glucose. — [Covington](https://cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products); [Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/01/fda-updates-two-digital-health-final-guidance-documents); [Faegre Drinker](https://faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/1/key-updates-in-fdas-2026-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance); [FDA guidance page](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices) (primary, not re-fetched)
- The policy is enforcement discretion, not a safe harbour. Marketing claims remain decisive, and FTC, state AG and private-litigant exposure continues. — [Bloomberg Government](https://news.bgov.com/safety/fda-eases-wellness-device-regulation-but-marketing-is-still-key); [Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2026/01/fda-updates-its-general-wellness-device-frameworkwhat-companies-need-to-know)
- Under the guidance's long-standing framework, claims about "relaxation" or "stress management" are allowed general-wellness claims. Claims to treat or diagnose a disease, for example "treats anxiety disorder" or "treats insomnia", fall outside it. The 2016 and 2019 versions listed "managing stress" and "sleep management" as acceptable wellness categories. — [FDA guidance page](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices) (primary text, not re-fetched; verify the exact wording in the 2026 version)

**Indonesia**
- Kemenkes decree **KMK (Kepmenkes) No. 951/2026** sets guidance on marketing authorisation (izin edar) for software-based medical devices: SaMD, SiMD and AI-enabled devices, including LLMs. AI counts as a medical device only if it has a medical purpose. Whether authorisation is needed turns on "function and product claims". Clinical validation is required, and local validation applies where a product is developed or retrained on Indonesian-population data. Sources give different dates: signed or effective 7 September 2026, or 1 October 2026. — [SIP Law Firm](https://siplawfirm.id/id/publikasi/alat-kesehatan-berbasis-software-kepmenkes-951-2026); [Pure Global](https://www.pureglobal.com/news/indonesia-kmk-951-2026-software-medical-device-guideline); [Veritask](https://veritask.ai/en/artikel/minister-of-health-decree-no-951-2026-tightens-regulations-on-ai-based-medical-devices)
- Before KMK 951/2026, SaMD was handled case by case under the general medical-device framework, Permenkes 62/2017. — [rapath SaMD overview](https://rapath.github.io/id/digital-health/samd-overview/)
- BPOM covers drugs, food and cosmetics. Its advertising rules (drug promotion, BPOM Reg. 7/2026, effective 16 April 2026) do not directly cover a software wellness service, but they show the "objective, complete, not misleading" standard Indonesian regulators apply. — [Tilleke & Gibbins](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/indonesia-expands-rules-on-drug-promotion-and-advertising/6/); [Conventus Law](https://conventuslaw.com/report/indonesia-introduces-broader-rules-on-drug-promotion-and-advertising/)

### Inferences
- An "8-week bad to good" retreat framed as moving people from feeling bad to feeling good is ambiguous. If creators describe "bad" as anxiety, depression or insomnia, MHRA Example 2/6 logic applies and the product becomes a medical device (EU/UK class IIa or higher; US loses wellness discretion). A wellness-only intended-purpose statement, an exclusion of people with diagnosed conditions, and controls on creator copy are the main protection.
- Letting a creator's "philosophy" shape AI output means the creator also shapes the *product's* functionality and claims. Contracts should bar creators from medical framing and from recommending the programme to patients, and the company needs approval rights over their copy.
- PHQ-9 or GAD-7 style check-ins, if added to measure "progress", count as a device signal under MHRA guidance unless users with clinical scores are routed out.

### Gaps
- The full text of the 2026 FDA guidance's examples was not retrieved. Confirm that "stress management" and "sleep" examples remain listed.
- KMK 951/2026's risk classes and whether it covers wellness exemptions explicitly were not verified against the JDIH text.
- No EU MDCG document specific to mental-wellness apps was found beyond MDCG 2019-11.

## EU AI Act: obligations for AI that interacts with people or touches emotional or health topics; emotion-recognition limits; transparency; timelines

### Takeaway
A generative wellness assistant is most likely a **limited-risk system with Article 50 transparency duties** (disclose AI interaction, mark synthetic audio and text), applying from **2 August 2026**. It becomes high-risk only if it is a medical device under the MDR (Annex I), and the Digital Omnibus deal moved that date to **2 August 2028**. Prohibited practices (Art. 5) have applied since 2 February 2025. They include manipulative techniques that exploit vulnerabilities and emotion recognition in workplaces and education, which matters for the **employer channel**.

### Cited Findings
- Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act) entered into force on 1 August 2024. Art. 5 prohibitions and AI-literacy duties have applied since 2 February 2025, GPAI obligations since 2 August 2025, and most other provisions, including Art. 50 transparency, from 2 August 2026. — [EUR-Lex, Regulation 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) (primary text, not re-fetched)
- Art. 5(1)(f) bans AI systems that infer the emotions of a natural person in the **workplace and education institutions**, except for medical or safety reasons. Art. 5(1)(a)–(b) ban subliminal or manipulative techniques, and the exploitation of vulnerabilities due to age, disability or socio-economic situation, where these cause significant harm. — [EUR-Lex, Regulation 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) (primary text, not re-fetched)
- Art. 50: providers must design systems that interact with people so users are told they are dealing with AI, unless this is obvious. Providers of systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text must mark outputs in a machine-readable way. Deployers of emotion-recognition systems must inform the people exposed. — [EUR-Lex, Regulation 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) (primary text, not re-fetched)
- **Digital Omnibus on AI:** in November 2025 the Commission proposed postponing the high-risk obligations. A provisional agreement set **2 December 2027** for stand-alone Annex III high-risk systems and **2 August 2028** for AI in Annex I regulated products such as medical devices. Providers must still register systems in the EU database even when they consider them exempt from high-risk classification. — [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/rules-high-risk-ai-delayed-under-eu-omnibus-deal); [Gibson Dunn (27 May 2026)](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement); [Euronews (19 Nov 2025)](https://www.euronews.com/2025/11/19/european-commission-delays-full-implementation-of-ai-act-to-2027)
- **Status conflict:** Gibson Dunn (May 2026) said the deal still awaited formal adoption. Orrick (July 2026) describes the Omnibus as finalised and in force. — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement); [Orrick, July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/ja-JP/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)
- Art. 50 transparency obligations are "largely unaffected" by the Omnibus. The original proposal included a transitional period for **watermarking** of synthetic audio and text; whether the final text kept it is unverified. — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement); [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/publication/digital-omnibus-on-ai-adjusted-timelines-and-transitional-provisions-explained)

### Inferences
- Aurelia's input is self-reported text ("one sentence about how I feel"), not biometric data. That is probably not "emotion recognition" as the AI Act defines it, which is based on biometric data. Adding wearable or voice-based emotion inference would change this, and it would be **prohibited in an employer deployment** under Art. 5(1)(f) unless it falls within the medical/safety exception.
- For the employer channel: do not give employers individual mood data, and do not market the product as a way to "read" staff wellbeing.
- AI voice sessions need an AI disclosure and machine-readable marking of the synthetic audio from 2 August 2026 (EU users).

### Gaps
- The final Official Journal citation of the AI Omnibus, and whether Art. 50(2) watermarking got a grace period, were not confirmed.
- Commission guidelines on Art. 50 and the code of practice on AI-content marking were not retrieved.

## Advertising and endorsement rules for creators (FTC, EU UCPD/influencers, UK ASA/CAP, Indonesia) and substantiation of "% improvement" claims

### Takeaway
"+85.7% sleep improvement" and "stress −50%" are objective, quantified health-outcome claims. The US, UK and EU all require **competent and reliable scientific evidence held before the claim is made**. Mock or invented figures cannot be used in real marketing. Creators must disclose the paid relationship. In the US and, under the proposed EU Digital Fairness Act, in the EU, the **brand is responsible for its creators' compliance**. The UK ASA has already ruled against an AI mental-health app for implying clinical therapeutic effect.

### Cited Findings
**US**
- FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance (December 2022): health claims need "competent and reliable scientific evidence", generally high-quality randomised controlled human trials, and claims that sound specific (e.g. percentages) must be backed to that level. — [FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance) (primary text, not re-fetched)
- Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255), revised June 2023: clear and conspicuous disclosure of material connections; advertisers are liable for misleading endorser statements; endorsements by virtual or AI influencers and statements implying false independence are covered. — [FTC press release, June 2023](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/06/federal-trade-commission-announces-updated-advertising-guides-combat-deceptive-reviews-endorsements) (primary, not re-fetched); [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/social-justice/negative-influencing-pushes-ftc-rules-on-ads-and-testimonials)
- **Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule (16 CFR Part 465)**, final in August 2024 and **effective 21 October 2024**. It bans fake or AI-generated reviews and testimonials, including those attributed to people who do not exist or who did not have the experience; buying positive reviews; and undisclosed insider reviews. Civil penalties for knowing violations were $51,744 per violation at launch and **$53,088** as of the FTC's December 2025 warning letters. The FTC may count each fake testimonial separately. — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials); [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/08/22/2024-18519/trade-regulation-rule-on-the-use-of-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials); [FTC Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers); [Venable, Dec 2025](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2025/12/ftc-signals-heightened-enforcement-of-new)

**UK**
- CAP Code rule 12.1: objective claims about health must be backed by evidence, where relevant consisting of trials on people. Rule 12.2: marketers must not discourage essential treatment, and must not offer specific advice, diagnosis or treatment for conditions where medical supervision is required. — [CAP Code PDF](https://www.asa.org.uk/static/c6be0fb9-2c66-4248-ba5b824bf26fd3d3/fa3714eb-f3e9-4c45-b9d31f1b87d00d3a/The-CAP-Code.pdf)
- **ASA ruling, Antipadia Enterprises Ltd (AI mental-health app):** ads breached rules 12.1 and 12.2. The ASA read them as claiming therapeutic support for clinically relevant conditions, more effective than conventional therapy, and **used MHRA DMHT guidance to decide what counts as a medical purpose**. — [ASA ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/antipadia-enterprises-ltd.html)
- An ASA ruling against Oakever Games found that claims an app improved memory or **sleep** needed adequate substantiation, breaching rules 3.1, 3.7 and 12.1. — [ASA ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/oakever-games-pte-ltd-g24-1257433-oakever-games-pte-ltd.html)
- ASA advice on treating anxiety: claims to treat anxiety are treated as medical claims. — [ASA "Claims about treating anxiety"](https://asa.org.uk/news/claims-about-treating-anxiety.html)
- Commentary: the ASA "draws a hard line" on wellness brands making MHRA-regulated (medical) claims. — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/wellness-brands-under-scrutiny-asa-draws-a-hard-line-on-mhra-regulated-claims)

**EU**
- The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC) bans misleading actions and omissions. Annex I no. 11 bans undisclosed paid editorial content, no. 17 falsely claiming a product can cure illnesses, and no. 23b–c (added by the Omnibus Directive 2019/2161) fake or unverified consumer reviews. The Commission's Influencer Legal Hub sets out disclosure duties for creators. — [Commission Influencer Legal Hub](https://commission.europa.eu/business-economy-euro/product-safety-and-requirements/consumer-protection/influencer-legal-hub_en) (primary, not re-fetched); [UCPD, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02005L0029-20220528) (primary, not re-fetched)
- **Digital Fairness Act (DFA), proposed by the Commission around 11–13 November 2026; status: proposal, not law.** It defines influencer marketing as a commercial practice (Art. 3(15)), requires disclosure of commercial content, and bans influencer promotion of products that cannot lawfully be advertised. **Brands must ensure "contractually or by other appropriate means" that the influencer complies (Art. 11(6))**. It also blacklists ten dark patterns, requires express consent for free-trial-to-paid conversions, and requires easier cancellation. Reported fines reach up to 6% of turnover. — [Lewis Silkin, 11 Nov 2026](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/11/11/european-commission-publishes-long-awaited-digital-fairness-act-102o9b5); [CMS](https://cms.law/en/aut/legal-updates/inside-the-eu-s-planned-digital-fairness-act-digital-design-subscription-traps-and-influencer-marketing); [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/proposed-eu-digital-fairness-act-targets-addictive-design-dark-patterns-and-unfair); [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/eus-digital-fairness-act-would-let-brussels-fine-firms-up-to-6-of-turnover/)
- BEUC argues for joint liability of influencers, agencies and brands. — [BEUC checklist](https://www.beuc.eu/sites/default/files/publications/BEUC-X-2026-019_Checklist_Towards_the_Digital_Fairness_Act.pdf)

**Indonesia**
- No binding influencer-specific statute was found. The Indonesian advertising code (Etika Pariwara Indonesia, amended 2014) requires disclosure of paid endorsements but is self-regulatory and not legally binding. Academic commentary in 2025 calls for specific influencer rules. — [Universitas Islam Sultan Agung journal](https://jurnal.unissula.ac.id/index.php/akta/article/download/44812/pdf); [Atlantis Press INCLAR 2025](https://download.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/inclar-25/126018236)
- Sector regulators are adding disclosure rules: OJK (finance influencers must disclose paid promotions) and BPOM (seeking to rein in food and cosmetics influencers, March 2025). — [DealStreetAsia](https://www.dealstreetasia.com/stories/indonesias-ojk-financial-influencers-486700/); [Jakarta Post](https://www.thejakartapost.com/business/2025/03/13/bpom-wants-to-rein-in-food-cosmetic-influencers-to-prevent-chaos)
- The communications ministry (Kominfo, renamed Komdigi in 2024) can block non-compliant content and platforms; the 2023 TikTok Shop suspension is an example. This comes from a commercial compliance guide, so treat it with caution. — [AuditSocials](https://www.auditsocials.com/knowledge/regional-laws/indonesia-advertising-compliance)

### Inferences
- "+85.7% sleep improvement" and "stress −50%" are not usable in any of the four markets without an RCT-grade study of *this* product. Even with one, "stress" outcomes must stay in wellness terms (not "anxiety"). Aurelia's own figures are invented mock data, which makes using them in real ads deceptive per se.
- AI-generated "member stories" or testimonials would breach the FTC Reviews Rule and the UCPD Annex I.
- The creator contract should cover: approved claims only; mandatory #ad / paid-partnership disclosure; no medical framing; takedown on request; indemnity; and audit rights. Under DFA Art. 11(6) and the FTC Endorsement Guides, the brand is expected to monitor its creators.

### Gaps
- The date and full text of the Antipadia ruling were not checked.
- No ASA or FTC action specific to "% improvement" claims by a meditation or sleep app was retrieved.
- The current Indonesian rule on online advertising disclosure (Komdigi or the Consumer Protection Law 8/1999) was not verified.

## Health data (GDPR Art. 9, FTC HBNR and the BetterHelp/GoodRx/Cerebral cases, Washington MHMDA, Indonesia PDP Law): what changes when a creator or employer is a data partner?

### Takeaway
A sentence about how someone feels, plus mood or sleep check-ins, is **health data** under GDPR Art. 9, "consumer health data" under Washington's MHMDA, "specific personal data" under Indonesia's PDP Law, and "PHR identifiable health information" under the FTC's HBNR. The US enforcement pattern is that **sharing health signals with ad platforms or partners without express consent is the violation**. Handing creators or employers identifiable user-level data is the highest-risk design choice.

### Cited Findings
- GDPR Art. 9 bans processing of data concerning health unless an exception applies, typically explicit consent (Art. 9(2)(a)). Art. 35 requires a DPIA for large-scale processing of special-category data. Art. 26 joint controllers must have an arrangement that sets out their respective responsibilities. — [GDPR, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) (primary text, not re-fetched)
- **GoodRx (February 2023):** the FTC's first Health Breach Notification Rule action. It brought a **$1.5M civil penalty** and banned sharing health data with third parties for advertising. — [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/b/ftc-proposes-1-5m-fine-for-violation-of-health-breach-notification-rule); [Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/ftcs-one-two-punch-on-data-tracking-and-health-privacy)
- **BetterHelp (March 2023), online therapy:** **$7.8M** for sharing mental-health questionnaire data and email addresses with Facebook, Snapchat and others for advertising, despite privacy promises. It was brought under FTC Act §5 (deception/unfairness), not the HBNR. The money went to consumer refunds. — [Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/teladoc-owned-betterhelp-pay-78m-users-alleged-data-misuse-ftc-order); [Arnold & Porter](https://arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/enforcement-edge/2023/03/ftc-announces-betterhelp-fine); [Crowell](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/ftc-enforcement-against-sharing-consumer-health-information-continues)
- **Cerebral (April 2024), online mental health:** a proposed order of about $7M in total. It comprised about $5.1M in refunds for **deceptive negative-option and cancellation practices**, plus a $10M civil penalty suspended after $2M was paid. Cerebral shared data on about 3.2M consumers with third parties through tracking pixels, and the order brings a first-of-its-kind ban on using health data for most advertising. It required court approval in S.D. Fla.; the former CEO was litigated separately. — [FTC press release](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/proposed-ftc-order-will-prohibit-telehealth-firm-cerebral-using-or-disclosing-sensitive-data); [STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2024/04/15/ftc-fines-cerebral-telehealth-health-data-sharing/); [The Markup](https://themarkup.org/pixel-hunt/2024/04/22/cerebral-to-pay-7-million-fine-and-limit-health-data-use-for-ads-under-federal-order)
- The FTC amended the HBNR in April 2024 (effective July 2024) to state explicitly that it covers health apps, and that an unauthorised disclosure (not only a hack) is a "breach of security". — [FTC press release, April 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-changes-health-breach-notification-rule) (primary, not re-fetched)
- **Washington My Health My Data Act (effective 31 March 2024 for most entities):** consumer health data is defined broadly. Consent is needed to collect, a separate consent to share, and a signed authorisation to sell. There is a **private right of action** through the Washington Consumer Protection Act (actual damages, attorney's fees, possible trebling). The first class action, *Maxwell v. Amazon.com*, No. 2:25-cv-00261 (W.D. Wash.), was filed on 10 February 2025 over an SDK in third-party apps. Connecticut, Nevada and New York have similar laws without a private right of action. — [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250220-first-lawsuit-filed-under-washingtons-my-health-my-data-act); [Proskauer](https://privacylaw.proskauer.com/2025/03/articles/privacy-law/my-health-my-dollar-amazons-health-data-troubles-in-washington/); [Washington State Standard](https://washingtonstatestandard.com/2025/02/21/lawsuit-against-amazon-provides-first-test-of-was-health-data-privacy-law/)
- **Indonesia PDP Law (UU 27/2022):** enacted 17 October 2022 with a two-year transition, so full compliance was due by **17 October 2024**. Health data is "specific personal data". The law provides administrative fines, criminal penalties and a private right of action. Implementing regulations and the supervisory agency were still pending, with some 2025-era sources expecting them in 2025. The Health Law (UU 17/2023) adds a duty to notify data owners of failures to protect health data. — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/int/legal-updates/compliance-with-indonesia-s-personal-data-protection-law-by-october-2024); [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/indonesias-personal-data-protection-bill-overview-key-takeaways-and-context/); [Tilleke & Gibbins](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/personal-data-protection-and-healthcare-services-in-indonesia/2/); [Chambers 2026](https://chambers.com/practice-guides/guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/indonesia/law-and-practice)

### Inferences
- **Creator as data partner:** if creators receive identifiable buyer data (names, mood or progress), they likely become a GDPR controller or joint controller. That requires an Art. 26 arrangement, explicit consent naming them, and a DPIA. Under the MHMDA it is a "sharing" needing separate consent, and under the HBNR an unconsented disclosure is a "breach". The safer design gives creators only **aggregate, de-identified sales and engagement metrics**.
- **Employer as partner:** aggregate-only reporting with minimum cohort sizes. Employee consent is generally not "freely given" under GDPR because of the power imbalance, and in the EU this sits next to the AI Act's ban on workplace emotion inference.
- No ad pixels or SDKs (Meta, TikTok, Google) on screens that capture feelings or check-ins. This is the exact fact pattern in BetterHelp, GoodRx, Cerebral and Maxwell.
- The 2% of annual revenue maximum administrative fine in the PDP Law (Art. 57) comes from training knowledge, not from a source found this session. Verify it.

## Payments and marketplace: Stripe Connect splits, platform liability for creators' content, EU 14-day withdrawal for digital services, VAT/OSS

### Takeaway
A 60/40 split through Stripe Connect makes Aurelia a marketplace or platform operator. Who is the "merchant of record" and the "trader" decides who owes consumer refunds, VAT and claim liability. For EU consumers, a 14-day withdrawal right applies unless express consent and acknowledgement are obtained before supply starts. Cancellation and negative-option practices are an active FTC enforcement theme, as the Cerebral case shows.

### Cited Findings
- EU Consumer Rights Directive 2011/83/EU: 14-day withdrawal right for distance contracts. For digital content not supplied on a tangible medium, the right is lost only if the consumer gave **prior express consent** to begin performance within the withdrawal period **and acknowledged** losing the right, and the trader confirmed this on a durable medium (Art. 16(m), as amended by Directive 2019/2161). For digital *services*, the consumer may owe a proportionate amount for the service already provided, if they requested an early start. — [Directive 2011/83/EU consolidated, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02011L0083-20220528) (primary text, not re-fetched)
- The proposed DFA would require express consent for free-trial-to-paid conversions and simpler cancellation of digital subscriptions (status: proposal, November 2026). — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/aut/legal-updates/inside-the-eu-s-planned-digital-fairness-act-digital-design-subscription-traps-and-influencer-marketing)
- The Cerebral order required an easy cancellation method and an end to misrepresenting negative-option terms; about $5.1M of the money was refunds for cancellation practices. — [Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/regulatory/cerebral-agrees-7m-settlement-ftc-doj-over-user-data-sharing-cancellation-practices)
- EU VAT: B2C electronically supplied services are taxed where the customer is, and the One-Stop Shop (OSS) lets one registration cover all EU states. Under Art. 9a of Implementing Regulation 282/2011, a platform intervening in the supply of e-services is generally presumed to be acting in its own name, so it is the deemed supplier for VAT, unless strict conditions are met. — [European Commission OSS page](https://vat-one-stop-shop.ec.europa.eu/index_en) (primary, not re-fetched); [Implementing Regulation 282/2011, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02011R0282-20250101) (primary, not re-fetched)
- Under Stripe Connect, the platform chooses a charge type: destination charges or "separate charges and transfers", where the platform is the merchant of record and bears refunds and chargebacks; or direct charges, where the connected account (the creator) is. — [Stripe Connect charges docs](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/charges) (primary, not re-fetched)
- EU Digital Services Act (Regulation 2022/2065), fully applicable from 17 February 2024: hosting providers keep their liability exemption for user content (Art. 6) unless they have knowledge and fail to act. Online platforms allowing B2C distance contracts carry trader-traceability duties (Art. 30). Art. 6(3) removes the exemption under consumer law where the platform presents the item so that an average consumer believes the platform itself supplies it. — [DSA, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj) (primary text, not re-fetched)

### Inferences
- Aurelia's AI generates the creator-flavoured sessions and Aurelia's branding presents them. Aurelia is therefore unlikely to be a "mere host" for the content. It should expect to be treated as the trader or supplier for consumer-law, VAT (Art. 9a) and claims purposes, and should own refunds and withdrawal flows centrally.
- Creators in the 60/40 split will need KYC as Stripe connected accounts. Payouts to Indonesian creators depend on Stripe's supported countries and payout rails. Not verified; see gaps.
- An 8-week programme sold as a one-off €19.99 is simpler than a subscription. If it auto-renews, US negative-option rules (Cerebral; state auto-renewal laws) and the DFA apply.

- Whether Stripe supports Indonesian connected accounts and payouts was not checked.

## Safety: crisis and self-harm handling expectations for AI mental-wellness products (Character.AI/Woebot events, 2025 state laws in Illinois, Utah, Nevada, California SB 243, NHS/NICE EVAs)

### Takeaway
The minimum expectations are now set by statute and litigation: AI disclosure; a documented suicide and self-harm protocol with crisis-line referral; no claim to provide therapy; and, in Illinois and Nevada, **no AI that provides or represents itself as providing mental-health therapy**. Character.AI and Google settled the teen-suicide suits in January 2026. A product sold as an 8-week mood-improvement "retreat" needs a crisis-detection and escalation flow, age gating, and copy that keeps clear of "therapy".

### Cited Findings
- **Illinois HB 1806, Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources Act, signed in August 2025:** bans AI from providing therapy or psychotherapy, or making therapeutic decisions, without a licensed professional. Licensed providers may use AI for administrative and supplementary support. Fines are up to **$10,000 per violation**. Commentators note uncertainty about how "wellness" apps are treated. — [TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/healthtechanalytics/news/366628773/Illinois-1st-US-state-to-ban-AI-in-mental-healthcare); [AEI](https://ctse.aei.org/illinois-bans-ai-therapy-questions-about-enforcement-remain/); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-ca/insights/publications/2025/08/ai-mental-health-chatbots)
- **Nevada (AB 406, 2025):** bars offering AI systems programmed to provide services "that would constitute the practice of professional mental or behavioral health care if provided by a natural person", and bars representing that AI can provide such care. Civil penalties are up to **$15,000**. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-ca/insights/publications/2025/08/ai-mental-health-chatbots); [The Batch](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/illinois-follows-nevada-prohibiting-certain-uses-of-chatbots-unless-used-by-licensed-therapists)
- **Utah HB 452 (2025):** regulates "mental health chatbots" rather than banning them. It requires disclosure that the user is interacting with AI, restricts advertising inside chats, and limits the sale or sharing of users' individually identifiable health information. — [Health Journalism](https://healthjournalism.org/blog/2025/08/states-crack-down-on-ai-for-behavioral-health-care); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-ca/insights/publications/2025/08/ai-mental-health-chatbots); [Utah HB 452 text](https://le.utah.gov/~2025/bills/static/HB0452.html) (primary, not re-fetched)
- **California SB 243 (companion chatbots), signed on 13 October 2025 and effective 1 January 2026:** operators must disclose AI status, maintain a protocol to prevent suicidal ideation, suicide and self-harm content that refers users to crisis services, apply extra protections for minors, and **report annually** on crisis referrals from 1 July 2027. A private right of action is reported. Follow-on bill SB 300 was heard in January 2026. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/13/california-becomes-first-state-to-regulate-ai-companion-chatbots); [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law); [bill text, LegiScan](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/SB243/2025)
- **Character.AI:** *Garcia v. Character Technologies* concerned the February 2024 suicide of 14-year-old Sewell Setzer III. A notice of resolution filed on about 7 January 2026 showed Character.AI, its founders and Google agreed to settle this and related suits in Florida, Colorado, New York and Texas. The terms were undisclosed and court approval was pending. — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/character-ai-google-agree-to-settle-teen-chatbot-harm-lawsuits); [AOL/CNN syndication](https://www.aol.com/articles/character-ai-google-agree-settle-231917636.html); [Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1965918)
- **UK:** the MHRA published adverse-event reporting work for DMHTs in 2025 and updated its SaMD adverse-incident guidance after the post-market surveillance regulation changes. — [MHRA AE reporting report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/report-on-adverse-event-ae-reporting-in-digital-mental-health-technologies-dmhts-and-the-development-of-updates-to-regulatory-guidance/report-on-adverse-event-ae-reporting-in-digital-mental-health-technologies-dmhts-and-the-development-of-updates-to-regulatory-guidance); [MHRA newsletter, Nov 2025](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/UKMHRA/bulletins/3fc4a48)
- **NICE early value assessments:** HTE30, digital front door technologies for NHS Talking Therapies (updated July 2025), and a 2026 assessment (HTE10055) where evidence existed only for Limbic Access and Wysa. HTG675 covers guided digital therapies for adult depression. NICE's first rapid healthtech guidance recommended digital CBT for 5–18-year-olds with mild–moderate anxiety or low mood, conditional on NHS England **DTAC** approval. A 2026 NICE-authored paper describes DMHT evidence quality as variable. — [NICE HTE30](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/HTE30/chapter/5-update-information); [NICE HTG675](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/htg675); [NICE news](https://www.nice.org.uk/news/articles/digital-mental-health-tech-for-children-and-young-people-recommended-by-nice-in-first-rapid-healthtech-guidance); [JMIR Mental Health 2026](https://mental.jmir.org/2026/1/e85635/PDF)

### Inferences
- In the US, Aurelia and its creators should never use "therapy", "therapist", "treats anxiety" or "replaces your counsellor". In Illinois and Nevada that language risks per-violation penalties as well as device and advertising issues. A creator's "philosophy" persona must not present as a clinician.
- Because the input is a free-text sentence about feelings, users will type crisis content. The expected baseline, drawn from SB 243 and the Character.AI settlements, is: detect self-harm signals; interrupt generation; show localised crisis resources (988 in the US, Samaritans 116 123 in the UK, 112 or national lines in the EU, Indonesia's 119 ext. 8); log for audit; apply an age gate or minor protections; and keep the creator's persona from overriding safety behaviour.
- NHS adoption (EVA/DTAC) is only relevant if Aurelia goes for a clinical claim, which would also make it a medical device. The wellness route and the NHS route are mutually exclusive positions.
